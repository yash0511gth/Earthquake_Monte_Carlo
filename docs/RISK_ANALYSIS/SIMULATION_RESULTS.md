# Simulation Results

## 1. Simulation Overview

The study created a Monte Carlo earthquake-loss simulation based on historical earthquake frequency data from the United States and established financial loss assumptions. A Poisson frequency model was employed to simulate annual earthquakes across magnitude groups, and uniform severity distributions were used to generate losses within the specified ranges. These components were combined into a modeling engine to generate potential annual earthquake-loss scenarios.

The model generated 10,000 simulated earthquake years, with each representing a potential annual loss scenario for the hypothetical insurer. The summary statistics, risk measures, and visualizations presented in this section are based on the resulting losses, measured in billions of dollars.

## 3. Metric Interpretation

The simulated annual losses have a **mean of $139.20 billion** and a **median of $125.67 billion**. This places the central simulated outcomes in the general range of $100–$170 billion, which is further reflected by the 25th percentile of $98.91 billion and the 75th percentile of $169.60 billion. In other words, half of the simulated years fall within this $70.69 billion interval, providing a clearer representation of the central portion of the simulated outcomes than the mean alone.

The simulation covers a total observed loss range of **$42.92 billion to $498.37 billion**. The difference between the central outcomes and the upper end of the range indicates that the simulation produces substantially larger annual losses beyond the central portion of the distribution. The 95th percentile of $244.54 billion and the 99th percentile of $308.64 billion provide further benchmarks for these less common outcomes. Approximately 5% of simulated years exceed $244.54 billion, while approximately 1% exceed $308.64 billion.

The tail-risk measure provides another perspective on the higher-loss portion of the distribution. At the selected **$200 billion threshold**, the simulation produces an exceedance probability of **13.62%**, meaning that approximately 1,362 of the 10,000 simulated years exceed this amount. The tail-risk curve extends this measure beyond a single threshold by showing how the exceedance probability changes as the loss threshold increases.

Taken together, these measures demonstrate how the 10,000 simulated outcomes are distributed across central, broad-range, high-loss, and tail-loss levels.

## 4. Visualization of Simulation Results

The four visualizations offer complementary perspectives on the simulated annual-loss distribution. The **histogram** demonstrates that the simulated losses are concentrated in the lower and middle portions of the distribution, while the distribution extends toward substantially larger losses. This pattern is consistent with the difference between the mean and median and the increasing loss levels observed at the upper percentiles.

The **boxplot** shows that the central 50% of the simulation ranges between $98.91 billion and $169.60 billion, with a median of $125.67 billion. The observed range extends from $42.92 billion to $498.37 billion, demonstrating the broader range of outcomes generated by the simulation beyond the central portion of the distribution.

The **CDF** presents a cumulative view of these outcomes, showing how the proportion of simulated years increases as the annual loss level rises. The curve provides a visual representation of the percentile results reported above, including the loss levels associated with the 95th and 99th percentiles.

The **tail-risk curve** focuses on the upper portion of the distribution. It depicts the decrease in exceedance probability as the loss threshold increases and provides visual context for the 13.62% probability of exceeding $200 billion reported in the tail-risk metrics.

Taken together, the visualizations support the numerical results reported in this section and provide a graphical representation of the range, concentration, and upper-loss behavior of the 10,000 simulated annual outcomes.
